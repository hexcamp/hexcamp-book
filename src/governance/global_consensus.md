# Global Consensus

The Hex.Camp project is primarily an "open source" project.

Each community, such as vanhex.ca or seahex.org is controlled and run by people in their respective areas.

However, there is some coordination between communities to ensure that the geographic footprint of two communities will never overlap.

There will be a centralized registry of communities and geographic footprints, defined by the H3 hexagons that are allocated to them.

It will be possible to lookup from the registry database which community "owns" any H3 indexed hexagon. Since the communities do not overlap, there will only be one owner community for any hexagon.

Initially, communities will be granted ownership of their geographic footprints by the organization that controls the central registry.

## Peerhex.org

Because we want to have communities for every city in the world, and each community should have their own domain name, it would be very expensive to have to purchase a separate domain name for every city.

By using subdomains of peerhex.org, we can create domain names for communities that are new (with few or no users yet) and in an incubation state. For any particular city that we wish to incubate, we will choose domain names that use well-known airport codes
corresponding to the city.

eg. An incubator community for London, England could use lhr.peerhex.org as their domain name. (LHR = London Heathrow Airport). Once there are enough people involved and they are willing to crowd-fund purchasing a domain name, the community could switch to a new domain.

## Wide-area communities

Additionally, just to ensure that any hexagon ID on the planet has an associated community, even if it happens to be in the middle of the ocean (or in Antarctica), we have defined three "fallback" communities that each cover a third of the planet:

* americas.peerhex.org - North and South America
* emea.peerhex.org - Europe, Middle-East and Africa
* asia.peerhex.org - Asia

## Disputed political boundaries, war and despotic regimes

Since the Hex.Camp registry covers the entire world, it will eventually need policies to deal with communites that affected by disputed political boundaries, the effects of wars, and communities that have to live within despotic political regimes.

## Boundaries

We will tend to follow patterns of human interconnection when decided what communities get which hexagons. Many metropolitan areas are situated across national boundaries. For these interconnected places, it would make sense to have a single community defined instead of multiple communities split by national boundaries. For example, Basel is Switzerland, but spreads out over the borders into France and also Germany.

For some smaller countries, it might make sense to model the entire country as a single community. For larger countries, we will try to avoid modelling a hierarchy of federal, province/state and county level communities. We'd prefer a flatter representation of communities based on human population and geography.

Much of the world lives in cities that are part of a "megalopolis" ... there are many cities nearby that merge into each other:

* [Wikipedia List of Megalopolises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_megalopolises)

When communities are in the "incubating" state, their boundaries will be somewhat fluid. As the communities mature, part of the work will be to define what their "footprint" should be. They will be expected to work together with their neighbouring communities to decide how gets what hexagon. The division should be based on human interconnection, although political and physical boundaries can sometimes be helpful.

Hex.Camp has coverage for the entire geography of Earth, so it also covers all of the rural areas. Smaller villages and towns may not feel like they are part of an urban community, but instead feel kinship for other small villages and towns in their region. As they are small, there might not be enough people form a "critical mass" of users for just their own town or village, but by banding together with other settlements in their region, there can be enough people to sustain a rural Hex.Camp community with a geographic footprint that covers a wide area. For definiging rural communities that have a wide area, political boundaries are probably quite helpful.
